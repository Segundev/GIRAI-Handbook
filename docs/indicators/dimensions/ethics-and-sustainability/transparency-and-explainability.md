Dimension: Ethics and Sustainability Indicator : Transparency and Explainability

=== "Definition"

    * [Transparency](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/transparency) is defined as ‘the quality of \[a process, practice or decision\] being done in an open way without secrets so people can trust that \[actions\] are fair and honest’. Transparency has been identified by the UN Human Rights Council (UNHRC) as one of the [five key principles](https://www.ohchr.org/en/good-governance/about-good-governance) of good governance, along with responsibility, accountability, participation and responsiveness to public concerns.

    * AI transparency refers to the degree of accessibility and openness of an AI system's internal mechanisms, necessitating the provision of comprehensible explanations of algorithms, models, datasets, and rationale for their [use](https://ethics-of-ai.mooc.fi/chapter-4/2-what-is-transparency). It also refers to transparency in their capabilities and use whether by a public or private actor to ensure that people understand these systems, including the reasons for their development, training, deployment, and impact.

    * Accordingly, the principle of transparency implies the concept of [explainability](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12525-022-00603-6#:~:text=According%20to%20Gilpin%20et%20al,of%20their%20decisions%E2%80%9D%20\(p.), which refers to ‘the quality of enabling people to understand how a particular system works and/or how a particular outcome was achieved by providing information that is sensible and easy-to-understand’. The principle of explainability is also closely linked to the concept of [interpretability](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00069), which refers to ‘the capacity to provide an explanation of the factors and logic that led to an outcome in terms that are understandable to a human.’ Transparency and explainability, as principles of [responsible AI governance](https://www.ohchr.org/en/good-governance/about-good-governance), therefore require information about processes, practices and decisions to be open, [accessible](https://web-archive.oecd.org/2012-06-17/59756-opennessandtransparency-pillarsfordemocracytrustandprogress.htm), and easily understood by a broad range of stakeholders, including government entities, private sector companies, civil society organisations and academic institutions.

=== "Why measure this indicator?"

    * Given AI's growing role in predictions, recommendations, and decisions, transparency regarding its use, functionality, and impact is vital. Understanding AI's reasoning, deployment, processes, and training data is crucial due to potential risks. As shown in the [UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000381137), a lack of transparency can hinder the ability to challenge AI-based decisions, affecting fair trials and access to redress. Conversely, increased transparency allows individuals to understand AI system operations, identify influencing factors, and verify the presence of safeguards like safety and fairness measures.

    * The OECD states that transparency in AI requires [disclosing how AI systems](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P7) are used, especially when making predictions or recommendations, or when people interact with AI agents. This involves providing easily understandable information on AI system development, training, operation, and deployment, enabling informed decisions. Additionally, the OECD emphasizes the importance of open dialogue among stakeholders and dedicated entities to raise AI awareness, address public concerns, and build confidence in responsible AI application.

=== "What to look out for"

    This indicator measures mechanisms (frameworks and government led initiatives) countries  have taken to promote and ensure sufficient levels of transparency and explainability in the development, use and deployment of AI technologies. Some key areas this indicator would assess include

    * The promotion of clarity regarding how AI systems operate, including their purpose, data sources, and decision-making logic, to foster public trust. Examples: (i) Public register of high-risk AI systems, detailing purpose, training data, and performance metrics; (ii) insisting that user-facing AI tools (e.g., medical diagnostics) must summarize influencing factors like symptoms or lab results; (iii) Promoting open-source AI for public and research scrutiny of code and logic.

    * The prevention of "black box" AI where the internal workings are opaque, making it difficult to understand how and why a decision was reached. Examples: (i) Mandatory explainability standards; (ii) requiring independent third-party audits of AI systems to verify their decision-making processes; and (iii) prohibiting the use of highly complex, unexplainable AI models in contexts where their decisions could have a significant impact on an individual's life, such as in child welfare risk assessments.

    * The assignment of obligations to entities developing and using AI to provide meaningful explanations of their systems' outputs, especially in high-stakes contexts like hiring or loan applications. Examples: (i) Obligate financial institutions to provide a clear, easy-to-understand explanation to loan applicants if their application is denied by an AI system, detailing the key factors that contributed to the decision; (ii) Require employers to explicitly inform job candidates if an AI system is being used to screen their resumes or conduct initial interviews; (iii) Mandate that companies using AI in areas like content moderation publish regular transparency reports detailing how their AI systems operate and the types of content they remove.

    * The establishment of rights for individuals to receive an explanation for a decision made by an AI system that affects them, and to understand the key factors that led to that outcome. Examples: (i) Grant individuals the right to request the specific data points an AI system used to make a decision about them, such as a credit score report; (ii) Implement a user-friendly process where individuals can automatically request and receive an explanation for a decision; (iii) Guarantee an individual's right to appeal an AI-driven decision and have it reviewed by a human who can access the explanation provided by the AI and make an independent judgment.

=== "Examples"

    - **Government Frameworks**<br>
    These could be laws, regulations, policies (published or draft form), and/or guidelines. They could recommend or require the government and  non-state actors to advance transparency and explainability in AI. Start by identifying any binding law or regulation that aims to govern AI. Note the different terms often synonymous with transparency regarding AI such as ‘notice’, ‘disclosure’, and ‘black box’. Look for evidence of specific regulatory requirements that call for notice requirements or specific policy recommendations in relation to transparency. Transparency requirements may be proportional to perceived risk levels. AI systems may also be referred to as ‘algorithmic systems’, and it may be useful to include ‘machine learning’ in searches.<br><br>
    For example, New Zealand enforces AI transparency and explainability through frameworks like the [Artificial Intelligence and Information Privacy Principles](https://data.govt.nz/toolkit/data-ethics/government-algorithm-transparency-and-accountability/algorithm-charter/) (IPP). IPP, for example, requires agencies to disclose collected information and its usage, applying to AI by mandating understanding of training data and development processes. For AI tools screening documents, transparency is crucial before reliance.

    - **Government-led Initiatives**<br>
    These could include concrete government actions, projects or programs approved or operational during the study timeframe, funded, and managed by a government entity to advance transparency and explainability in the context of AI. It may also include the establishment of an oversight body mandated to monitor compliance with principles related to the indicator, the creation of an expert working group to develop policies or programmes.<br><br>
    Ireland [established](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-enterprise-tourism-and-employment/campaigns/artificial-intelligence-ai-advisory-council/) a national AI Advisory Council in January 2024 to provide independent expert advice to the government on artificial intelligence policy. The AI Advisory Council is [delegated](https://assets.gov.ie/static/documents/Artificial_Intelligence_AI_Advisory_Council__Terms_of_reference.pdf) to provide expert insights advisory to the government on emerging opportunities and challenges regarding AI, as well as engaging in public relations to promote trustworthy, ethical and human-centred AI. To foster public trust in AI, the Council [recommends](https://enterprise.gov.ie/en/publications/publication-files/ai-advisory-council-recommendations-helping-to-shape-irelands-ai-future.pdf) that the government adopt responsible AI innovation, stressing the importance of public transparency and accountability, including implementing legislative and accountability frameworks to ensure accurate, fair, and transparent AI applications, as well as regular independent auditing particularly for technologies such as facial recognition.

=== "Research Guidance"

    When reviewing sources, look for terms like 'algorithmic systems,' 'automated systems,' and 'algorithmic/automated decisions,' as they may indicate discussions about transparency and explainability, even if AI isn't explicitly mentioned. Search the responsible ministry website (e.g. Ministry of Economics, Ministry of Technology, Ministry of Justice, etc.) and identify events, monitoring mechanisms or information related to transparency and explainability. Check if there might be guidelines specifying the application of general laws (consumer protection etc.) to the use-case of AI that impose specific notice requirements or dissemination specifications to ensure adequate explainability.

    #### Useful Sources

    * Existing literature on the (complex) issue of transparency around AI/algorithmic systems, including new developments/reforms in the country and recent academic research (e.g. reports, policy briefs, news/articles, white papers, academic papers)
    * Websites of civil society/other non-governmental organisations promoting the issue of or addressing transparency and/or explainability in AI
    * OECD.AI (live repository of global AI policy initiatives)

    #### Search

    * Parliamentary or government records for recent mentions of ‘transparency and AI’, ‘disclosure and AI’, ‘explainable AI’ etc.
    * General google searches for ‘transparency and AI and \[country\]’, ‘explainability and AI and \[country\]’, etc. (can also include terms ‘policy’, ‘framework’, ‘law’, etc.)
    * Academic search engines ([Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/), [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/), etc.) for papers on ‘transparency and AI and \[country\]’, ‘explainability and AI and \[country\]’, etc.

    #### Consult

    * Civil society organisations in the country dealing with AI transparency and explainability such as disclosure, public participation, etc.
    * Academics/researchers specialising on transparency and explainable AI
    * Computer scientists developing or AI experts regarding frameworks/solutions supporting AI transparency/explainability
    * Businesses or corporations providing tools/resources in support of transparent and explainable AI
