Dimension: Inclusion and Diversity Indicator : Children’s Rights

=== "Definitions"

    * Children’s rights refer to the subset of human rights that recognises the need to provide children with [special care and protection](https://archive.crin.org/en/library/legal-database/un-declaration-rights-child-1959.html) given their dependence on adults for survival, protection, and development. A [‘child’](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child) is defined as anyone under the age of 18, or until they attain majority, or legal age, earlier under an applicable law.

    * The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child ([UNCRC](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child)) recognises the primary role of parents in safeguarding the rights of their children, it also places affirmative obligations on states to create the conditions for these responsibilities to be fulfilled. The UNCRC sets the global standard for children’s rights which can be broadly organised into four categories: (i) **Survival rights** which include the right to life and access to basic services such as food, shelter, adequate living standards, and medical services. (ii) **Development rights** which include the right to education, play, leisure, cultural activities, access to information, and freedom of thought, conscience and religion.(iii) **Protection rights** which include the right to be protected from all forms of abuse, neglect and exploitation. (iv) **Participation rights** which include the right to freedom of expression, to have a say in matters affecting their lives, and to join associations and to assemble peacefully.

    * In 2021, the UN’s Committee on the Rights of the Child published [General Comment No. 25](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/general-comment-no-25-2021-childrens-rights-relation) on ‘children’s rights in relation to the digital environment’, guiding states on implementing the UNCRC digitally. Similarly, UNICEF’s [AI for children](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/projects/ai-for-children) project developed [policy guidance](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children) on AI for children, based on the UNCRC, recommending how states and organizations can uphold children’s rights in AI policy and systems.

    * Various human rights instruments protect children's rights in the digital age, including AI contexts. These include the UN's [Optional Protocol](https://documents-dds-ny.un.org/doc/UNDOC/GEN/N00/625/67/PDF/N0062567.pdf?OpenElement) on the sale of children, child prostitution, and child pornography; the Council of Europe's [Convention on the Protection of Children against Sexual Exploitation and Sexual Abuse](https://rm.coe.int/1680084822); and the OECD's [Recommendation of the Council on Children in the Digital Environment](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/en/instruments/OECD-LEGAL-0389) and [Guidelines for Digital Service Providers](https://www.oecd.org/mcm/OECD%20Guidelines%20for%20Digital%20Service%20Providers.pdf). The [African Union Child Online Safety and Empowerment Policy](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/43798-doc-African_Union_Child_Online_Safety_and_Empowerment_Policy_Feb_2024.pdf) is another relevant non-binding instrument. While not specific to artificial intelligence, these instruments establish important protections for children in digital environments, including AI systems.

=== "Why measure this indicator?"

    * AI is increasingly present in children's daily lives, powering social media, apps, websites, and online games that offer recommendations and mediate [content](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/media/10726/file/Executive%20Summary:%20Memorandum%20on%20Artificial%20Intelligence%20and%20Child%20Rights.pdf). Similarly, there have been recent emergence of AI-enhanced toys, [designed](https://www.unicef.org/media/163786/file/2024-10_Blog%20ECD%20and%20AI_cw_zj_am.pdf.pdf) to provide interactive and personalised play experience. While potentially beneficial, unregulated AI use poses risks to children's rights to privacy, play, protection from exploitation and abuse, and  [non-discrimination](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/docserver/9e0e49a9-en.pdf?expires=1678184139&id=id&accname=guest&checksum=975A87487AD8B92C78FB3BA9BDE931C2) due to a lack of transparency. Children are also more vulnerable to harmful content and [advertising](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/docserver/9e0e49a9-en.pdf?expires=1678184139&id=id&accname=guest&checksum=975A87487AD8B92C78FB3BA9BDE931C2) in the digital environment.

    * A 2020 [analysis](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/docserver/9e0e49a9-en.pdf?expires=1678184139&id=id&accname=guest&checksum=975A87487AD8B92C78FB3BA9BDE931C2) of national strategies on protecting children online found that while some countries had legislative and policy responses, they were often "fragmented" and lacked a "comprehensive framework" or coordinated government response. Many countries face the challenge of promoting digital media use among youth while protecting them from risks. Government actions (or inaction) disproportionately affect children, making this area essential for assessing responsible [AI](https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/child-rights-why-they-matter).

=== "What to look out for"

    This indicator examines the measures countries have in place and/or are developing to ensure that the design, development and use of AI is happening in a way that upholds children’s rights. Some key areas this indicator would assess include:

    - **The protection of children's personal data and privacy when they interact with AI systems.**<br>
    Examples: requirements to anonymise and encrypt children’s data.

    - **The prevention of the use of AI to exploit, manipulate, or harm children, including through targeted advertising or inappropriate content.**<br>
    Examples: implementation of security measures including transparency about data use, restrictions around third-party sharing of children’s data.

    - **The assignment of obligations to developers and deployers of AI to design systems that are safe, age-appropriate, and beneficial for children.**<br>
    Examples: requirements for developers designing AI tools for children, guardians and schools to perform child-specific risk assessments.

    - **The establishment of rights for children, and their guardians, to seek redress if they are harmed by AI systems.**<br>
    Examples: requirements for child-accessible harm reporting mechanisms, prioritisation of addressing AI-related harms related to children, and legal remedies of AI-enabled harms toward children

=== "Examples"

    - **Government Frameworks**<br>
    These could be laws, regulations, policies (published or draft form), and/or guidelines. They could recommend or commit the government and non-state actors to protect children's rights from AI risks. These may be standalone or integrated into existing digital human rights frameworks. For example, Scotland's 2021 [AI Strategy](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/62cd519a0b49ae6dcee5dc8c/t/62d80552ad77dc084206e679/1658324316824/Scotlands_AI_Strategy_Web_updated_single_page_aps.pdf) acknowledges AI's impact on children and aims to establish policies empowering them in AI development and use. Particularly, the strategy outlines key principles for children and AI: protecting data and privacy, ensuring safety, providing transparency and accountability, educating governments and businesses on AI's impact on children's rights, and fostering child-centered AI. Similarly, the [Portuguese Charter of the Human Rights in the Digital Age](https://diariodarepublica.pt/dr/legislacao-consolidada/lei/2021-164870244%20) grants children the right to protection and special care, including safety in cyberspace and freedom of expression, all applicable to AI and its impact.The Charter specifically addresses the rights of children, including their entitlement to special protection and care for their well-being and safety in cyberspace as well as their rights to freedom of expression of information or ideas commensurate with their age and maturity. These rights, as stated in the Charter, extend to AI-related issues, particularly concerning its potential impact on children.

    - **Government-led Initiatives**<br>
    These could include concrete government actions, projects or programs approved or operational during the study timeframe, funded, and managed by a government entity to protect and / or promote children’s rights in the context of AI. It can be implemented by the government or in collaboration with other organizations, such as non-profits, private companies, or other countries. For this indicator, some examples include the United Kindgdom, which has collaborated with the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, [to actively combat online child sexual abuse](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/uk-to-target-those-profiting-from-child-sexual-abuse#:~:text=The%20UK%20is%20stepping%20up,AI%20and%20other%20emerging%20technologies), including cases involving AI. The country is working with its partners to develop advanced tools to identify AI-generated material and minimise law enforcement exposure to child sexual abuse images by using AI to the UK's child abuse image database. It has also engaged with tech companies supporting voluntary principles to counter online child sexual exploitation and abuse.

=== "Research Guidance"

    We recommend focusing on frameworks and measures that address rights specific to children in the context of AI. This includes a range of protections, such as their right to privacy and data protection, which encompasses the right not to be subjected to undue surveillance. It also extends to ensuring their safety from abuse and harm when interacting with AI, particularly on social media. This necessitates that children and their caregivers are included in the development of AI governance systems. Examine country policies/guidelines and measures to mitigate risks to children's rights and leverage benefits, and government actions (programs, interventions, monitoring/oversight) to implement them. If references to the latter but not the former exist, note this gap. Look for direct mentions of children's rights frameworks like the UNCRC and others mentioned above, and implementation measures. State-level frameworks might require private firms to meet data standards for children, offer remedies for violations (e.g., statutory liability for AI harm), or run awareness campaigns.

    #### Useful Sources

    * Existing literature on the issue of children’s rights with respect to digital technologies/platforms/AI, including on recent policy or implementation developments/reforms in the country and recent academic research (e.g. reports, policy briefs, news/articles, white papers, academic papers)
    * Case law related to the issue with respect to digital technologies/AI
    * Websites of civil society organisations involved in work around children’s rights and digital technologies/AI
    * Government initiatives, e.g. training or awareness programs to educate stakeholders, e.g. businesses/teachers/caregivers/government bodies on children’s rights in the digital age/with respect to AI
    * [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/overview) (live repository of global AI policy initiatives)

    #### Search

    * Parliamentary or government records for recent mentions of ‘children and digital technologies/AI’, etc.
    * General google searches for ‘children and AI and \[country\]’, ‘children and digital environment and \[country\]’, etc. (can also include terms ‘policy’, ‘report’, ‘law’, etc.)
    * Academic search engines ([Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/), [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/), etc.) for ‘children and AI and \[country\]’, ‘children and digital environment and \[country\]’,
    * Besides ‘children’ and ‘rights’, helpful alternative search terms may include ‘minors’, ‘youth’ and ‘risks’ and because frameworks may not explicitly reference AI, it may be productive to search for ‘digital environment’, ‘digital technologies’, ‘online platforms’, ‘digital platforms’, ‘internet’, ‘devices’, etc.

    #### Consult

    * Government officials (assess government efforts in promoting as well as upholding children’s rights with respect to digital technologies/AI)
    * AI developers (discuss presence/proposal of awareness programs around children’s rights on social media platforms with due regard to its risks)
    * Children’s rights advocates (explore frameworks and principles for protecting children’s rights with specific considerations for risks enhanced by digital technologies)
