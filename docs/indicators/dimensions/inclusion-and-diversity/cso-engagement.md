Dimension: Inclusion and Diversity Indicator: CSO Engagement on Inclusion and Diversity

=== "Definition"

    * To safeguard people's rights effectively, active civic participation is essential in ensuring accountability, amplifying marginalised voices, and representing the public interest in AI governance processes. In this regard, civil society organisations (CSOs) working in the field of artificial intelligence play a crucial role in actively shaping policies, implementing projects, raising public awareness, lobbying for policy formulation and implementation, and holding the government to account.

    * According to the [United Nations](https://www.un.org/en/get-involved/un-and-civil-society), “a civil society organisation (CSO) or non-governmental organisation (NGO) is any non-profit, voluntary citizens’ group which is organised on a local, national or international level”. These organisations focus on specific issues of public interest, such as human rights, poverty alleviation, inclusion, and inequality. The [EU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/glossary/civil-society-organisation.html) notes that “civil society refers to all forms of social action carried out by individuals or groups who are neither connected to nor managed by state authorities”, while CSOs are institutionalised or formal organisations “whose members serve the general interest through a democratic process and which plays the role of mediator between public authorities and citizens”.

    * CSOs operate through a variety of structures, including as charities, faith-based organisations, think tanks and research centres, trade unions, community groups, online networks, and social and recreational clubs. In the AI context,  the defining quality for a CSO is that the non-profit organisation engages in policy influencing, public awareness, research, knowledge production, advisory, advocacy or any other activity geared towards responsible development and deployment of AI.

=== "Why Measure this Indicator?"

    CSOs play a critical, sometimes outsized [role](https://www.globalcenter.ai/research/the-outsized-role-of-african-civil-society-and-universities-in-advancing-responsible-ai), in advancing responsible AI practices and policies. Data from the 2024 edition of the Global Index on Responsible AI identified more than 400 CSOs working to advance responsible AI across 19 thematic areas including gender equality, children's rights, and cultural and linguistic diversity.  Evidence of CSO engagement in these areas fill a crucial gap and offer a holistic assessment of the responsible AI ecosystem globally, given that in some contexts — such as Africa — CSO activities often [surpass](https://www.global-index.ai/Main-Findings) government-led efforts in responsible AI.

=== "What to look out for"

    The questionnaire for this indicator asks researchers to document evidence of CSO activities **across all indicators** (primary and secondary) in the Inclusion and Diversity dimension: *Gender Equality*, *Children’s Rights*, *Cultural and Linguistic Diversity*, and *Rights of Persons with Disability*. Researchers should therefore consider both the breadth of the evidence of initiatives as well as how well such evidence covers all the indicators. There are 6 CSO initiative types in the questionnaire:

    * Participation in public policy formulation, implementation, M\&E: as part of advisory councils, partnerships with state, independent observers, participating in parliamentary hearings
    * Litigation or legal action.
    * Capacity building and collaboration
    * Public engagement & awareness: conferences, public awareness events/campaigns, public consultations
    * Knowledge generation: research, technical reports.
    * Developing ethical guidelines, standards, and best practices

    You are to provide one evidence per initiative type and indicate which indicator(s) the evidence covers. It is very likely that initiatives are more concentrated on one indicator than others – this is fine, but we encourage researchers to ensure, where evidence is available, that each indicator has at least one evidence of a CSO initiative addressing it. Initiatives must be either ongoing or have taken place during the study timeframe (November 1, 2023 – September 30, 2025).

    You should only provide evidence that shows country-level engagement of CSO initiatives. Evidence from international CSOs are relevant, only if the initiative addresses your country's context and actual participation in the initiative from local partners could be demonstrated — this includes country chapters of international CSOs. For example, a [report](https://cdt.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Full-Text-Algorithm-driven-Hiring-Tools-Innovative-Recruitment-or-Expedited-Disability-Discrimination.pdf) by the Center for Democracy and Technology on the impact of algorithmic hiring tools on PWD applicants does not highlight any country context and would not count in this regard.

=== "Examples"

    Examples below are provided by initiative type:

    **Participation in public policy formulation, implementation, M\&E: as part of advisory councils, partnerships with state, independent observers, participating in parliamentary hearings**:

    In December 2024, in the UK, [5Rights Foundation](https://5rightsfoundation.com/), a non-profit organisation working on protecting the rights of children in the digital space, submitted [written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/132894/pdf) to the Science, Innovation and Technology Select Committee of the UK Parliament in response to the Committee’s call for evidence on the role that social media played in spreading misinformation during the UK 2024 riots, leading to the arrest of many — including some children. Being an organisation that works primarily on children, the 5Rights Foundation submitted written evidence addressing how social media algorithms fuel such misinformation.  One of the areas 5Rights Foundation addressed include discussing the roles that generative AI and LLMs play in creating and spreading harmful content and misinformation, including citing examples of AI-generated Child Sexual Abuse Material (CSAM) on social media platforms.

    **Litigation or legal action**

    In October 2024, in France, about 15 NGOs which include La Quadrature du Net, Amnesty International France, the Abbé Pierre Foundation, and the Human Rights League, have [filed a lawsuit](https://www.wired.com/story/algorithms-policed-welfare-systems-for-years-now-theyre-under-fire-for-bias/) against CNAF — the French government agency responsible for the administration of family-oriented social security system — asking for the agency to pause its use of social scoring algorithms due to concerns about widely known discriminatory practices against disabled people and single mothers.

    **Capacity building and collaboration**

    In October 2022, in the UK, the [Alan Turing Institute](https://www.turing.ac.uk/research/research-projects/exploring-childrens-rights-and-ai), together with the Scottish-based [Children’s Parliament](https://www.childrensparliament.org.uk/exploring-childrens-rights-and-ai/) and Scottish AI Alliance (SAIA), launched a three-phased, two-year-project called ‘[Exploring Children’s Rights in AI](https://www.scottishai.com/news/exploring-childrens-rights-in-ai)’ to investigate the relationship between primary school learners and AI. The project has hosted workshops in schools where basic concepts around AI and children’s human rights were taught and children’s voices were heard, in support of the non-discrimination principle with regard to children and AI. The Institute hosted the [Children’s AI Summit](https://www.turing.ac.uk/events/childrens-ai-summit) in April 2025, where the Children’s Manifesto for the Future of AI was [developed](https://www.turing.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-02/childrens_manifesto_for_the_future_of_ai.pdf) and later presented at the Paris Action Summit.

    **Public engagement & awareness: conferences, public awareness events/campaigns, public consultations:**

    In Uganda, [Pollicy](https://pollicy.org/), a non-profit organisation works to integrate AI into the larger feminist movement by promoting suitable data governance practices and advocating for responsible policies by engaging with women’s groups and gender minorities.

    **Knowledge generation: research, technical reports.**

    In April 2025, the [National Academy of Social Insurance](https://www.nasi.org/), a non-profit organisation, composed of social insurance professionals in the US, published a [report](https://www.nasi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Phase-One-Report-Task-Force-on-Artificial-Intelligence-Emerging-Technology-and-Disability-Benefits.pdf) titled “Task Force on Artificial Intelligence, Emerging Technology, and Disability Benefits”. The report was the product of a taskforce earlier set up to examine the Social Security Administration (US government agency)’s approaches to /use of AI in social security insurance and other schemes for PWDs. The report outlines risks with using AI in such administration, the current state of play in the use of algorithms for determining benefits for PWDs, and principles or guardrails to prevent harms.

    **Developing ethical guidelines, standards, and best practices**

    The American Foundation for the Blind has published a set of guiding principles toward development and deployment of more disability inclusive AI systems. The document discusses these rights in the USA context, where the Foundation operates — based on inputs from government, industry, civil society, and academic experts. The document, though [published](https://afb.org/sites/default/files/2025-02/Guiding%20Principles%20for%20More%20Inclusive%20AI.pdf) in February 2025, is [hosted as a set of live principles](https://www.afb.org/research-and-initiatives/empowering-or-excluding/guiding-principles-more-disability-inclusive-ai) (relevant beyond publication date) on the AFB’s website.

=== "Research Guidance"

    **The general rule is to prioritise** prominent organisations recognised for their engagement in AI ethics and governance, those contributing to public debates on the social implications of AI, those engaging in research and knowledge creation, those into policy advocacy or holding government to account, and those providing support for AI-related initiatives. This questionnaire collects evidence across **all indicators** (primary and secondary) in the Inclusion and Diversity dimension.

    Begin by scanning through the webpages of prominent CSOs working on AI in your country. From your search results, look out for other organisations collaborating in this area.

    #### **Useful Sources**

    * The websites of human rights agencies, civil society organisations, unions, and labour organisations or collectives that work on this dimension
    * Academic publications and reports from reputable (I)NGOs
    * Websites of credible news outlets
    * Government websites for evidence of engagements with civil society organisations.
    * Websites of and reports of UN Agencies and International Organisations (see e.g. UNESCO’s [website](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/es/node/48/list) of Global CSOs and Academic Network on AI Ethics and Policy)

    #### **Search**

    * News and articles about recent developments and reforms in relation to “AI” \+ “gender”, “children’s rights”, “rights of PWDs”, and “linguistic diversity” \+ “Country”
    * You can conduct speculative searches using the key words in each initiative type, e.g. “CSO organises capacity building on gender rights and AI in India” “Organisation takes legal action on protecting the rights of PWDs in Malaysia”
    * General google searches for ‘CSOs working on AI Inclusion and Diversity \[country\]’, ‘Gender equality and AI \[country\]’, etc. (can also include terms ‘NGOs’, ‘CSOs’, ‘Organisation’, etc.)
    * Academic search engines ([Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/), [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/), etc.) for ‘CSO reports on AI equality \[country\]

    #### **Consult**

    * Experts, civil society organisations and researchers working on digital rights and AI, specifically in relation to gender, children’s rights, PWDs, and inclusion.
