Dimension: Ethics and Sustainability Indicator: CSO Engagement on Ethics and Sustainability

=== "Definition"

    * To safeguard people's rights effectively, active civic participation is essential in ensuring accountability, amplifying marginalised voices, and representing the public interest in AI governance processes. In this regard, civil society organisations (CSOs) working in the field of artificial intelligence play a crucial role in actively shaping policies, implementing projects, raising public awareness, lobbying for policy formulation and implementation, and holding the government to account.

    * According to the [United Nations](https://www.un.org/en/get-involved/un-and-civil-society), “a civil society organisation (CSO) or non-governmental organisation (NGO) is any non-profit, voluntary citizens’ group which is organised on a local, national or international level” . These organisations focus on specific issues of public interest, such as human rights, poverty alleviation, inclusion, and inequality. The [EU](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/EN/legal-content/glossary/civil-society-organisation.html) notes that “civil society refers to all forms of social action carried out by individuals or groups who are neither connected to nor managed by state authorities”, while CSOs are institutionalised or formal organisations “whose members serve the general interest through a democratic process and which plays the role of mediator between public authorities and citizens”.

    * CSOs operate through a variety of structures, including as charities, faith-based organisations, think tanks and research centres, trade unions, community groups, online networks, and social and recreational clubs. In the AI context,  the defining quality for a CSO is that the non-profit organisation engages in policy influencing, public awareness, research, knowledge production, advisory, advocacy or any other activity geared towards responsible development and deployment of AI.

=== "Why Measure this Indicator"

    CSOs play a critical, sometimes outsized [role](https://www.globalcenter.ai/research/the-outsized-role-of-african-civil-society-and-universities-in-advancing-responsible-ai), in advancing responsible AI practices and policies. Data from the 2024 edition of the Global Index on Responsible AI identified more than 400 CSOs working to advance responsible AI across 19 thematic areas including gender equality, children's rights, and cultural and linguistic diversity.  Evidence of CSO engagement in these areas fill a crucial gap and offer a holistic assessment of the responsible AI ecosystem globally, given that in some contexts — such as Africa — CSO activities often [surpass](https://www.global-index.ai/Main-Findings) government-led efforts in responsible AI.

=== "What to look out for"

    The questionnaire for this indicator asks researchers to document evidence of CSO activities across **all indicators** (primary and secondary) in the Ethics and Sustainability dimension: *Fairness and non-discrimination*, *Transparency and Explainability*, *Human Oversight and Determination*, and *Environmental Impact*. Researchers should therefore consider both the breadth of the evidence of initiatives as well as how well such evidence covers all the indicators. There are 6 CSO initiative types in the questionnaire:

    * Participation in public policy formulation, implementation, M\&E: as part of advisory councils, partnerships with state, independent observers, participating in parliamentary hearings
    * Litigation or legal action.
    * Capacity building and collaboration
    * Public engagement & awareness: conferences, public awareness events/campaigns, public consultations
    * Knowledge generation: research, technical reports.
    * Developing ethical guidelines, standards, and best practices

    You are to provide one evidence per initiative type and indicate which indicator the evidence covers. It is very likely that initiatives are more concentrated on one indicator than others – this is fine, but we encourage researchers to ensure, where evidence is available, that each indicator has at least one evidence of a CSO initiative addressing it. Initiatives must be either ongoing or have taken place during the study timeframe (November 1, 2023 – September 30, 2025).

    You should only provide evidence that shows country-level engagement of CSO initiatives. Evidence from international CSOs are relevant, only if the initiative addresses your country's context and actual participation in the initiative from local partners could be demonstrated — this includes country chapters of international CSOs. A relevant example here is Amnesty International, an international CSO operating in more than 150 countries, which has led a [campaign](https://banthescan.amnesty.org/hyderabad/index.html) to ban facial recognition technologies in the Hyderabad city of India.

=== "Examples"

    Examples below are provided by initiative type:

    **Participation in public policy formulation, implementation, M\&E: as part of advisory councils, partnerships with state, independent observers, participating in parliamentary hearings**:

    KICTANet Kenya is a multistakeholder think tank working on digital policy and regulation. In January 2025, it [co-convened](https://www.kictanet.or.ke/shaping-the-future-of-ai-in-kenya/?) with the Kenyan Ministry of ICT & Digital Economy, a multistakeholder workshop on the Kenya AI Statement of Principles. The workshop fed into Kenya’s national AI Strategy, and emphasised core principles relating to the ethics & sustainability dimension, including human-centred design and accountability, transparency and public trust, and environmental sustainability.

    **Litigation or legal action.**

    In June 2025, the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) in the USA [filed](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/06/eff-nj-supreme-court-prosecutors-must-disclose-details-regarding-frt-used-identify) an amicus in the New Jersey Supreme Court in the case brought against the State of New Jersey by Mr Miles. The EFF urged the court to require prosecutors to disclose how facial-recognition tools work (source code, error rates etc) to defendants identified via Facial recognition technologies; a process that aims to strengthen transparency and explainability in AI systems.

    **Capacity building and collaboration**

    The Alan Turing Institute in the UK regularly offers training [courses](https://www.turing.ac.uk/courses) and workshops, and has published a publicly accessible [playbook](https://www.turing.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/aieg-ati-7-explainabilityv1.2.pdf) (2024) on transparency and explainability in AI.

    **Public engagement & awareness: conferences, public awareness events/campaigns, public consultations:**

    The U.S.-based [Algorithmic Justice League](https://www.ajl.org/) recognises AI’s potential for perpetuating different forms of discrimination as a result of bias and works to raise public awareness using a combination of art and research as well as to “equip advocates with resources to bolster campaigns, build the voice and choice of the most impacted communities, and galvanize researchers, policymakers, and industry practitioners to prevent AI harms.” Alongside its ongoing research and conferences, the organisation runs the multi-stakeholder project [Community Reporting of Algorithmic System Harms](https://www.ajl.org/crash-project) which allows individuals to report harms from algorithms and seek redress through the community.

    **Knowledge generation: research, technical reports.**

    In December 2024, Friends of the Earth Ireland in partnership with Prof. Hannah Daly at the University College Cork [published](https://www.friendsoftheearth.ie/assets/files/pdf/data_centrres_and_the_carbon_budgets_-_prof_hannah_daly_dec_2024.pdf?) a report that examines the impact of the growing number of data centres (a critical component of the AI industry) on Ireland’s energy and environmental landscape. The report shows empirical evidence demonstrating the data centre industry as a primary driver of energy usage in Ireland, significantly increasing CO2 emissions as well as other environmental and energy security risks. The report points out policy gaps and relevant recommendations to address them. The organisation has been [engaged](https://www.friendsoftheearth.ie/news/friends-of-the-earth-calls-on-irish-politicians-to-reject-th/) in broader advocacy work around AI’s environmental impact in Ireland and the EU

    **Developing ethical guidelines, standards, and best practices**

    In Argentina, in May 2025, the CIPPEC (Centro de Implementación de Políticas Públicas para la Equidad y el Crecimiento) [published](https://www.cippec.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Guia-para-el-uso-de-IA-en-el-sector-publico-en-Argentina_2025.pdf) the *Guía para el uso de IA en el sector público en Argentina*, a best-practice guide for AI adoption/use in public agencies. Chapter 7 of the guideline addresses requirements for transparency documentation and explanation across the AI lifecycle – including clear examples and implementation steps to follow.

=== "Research Guidance"

    **The general rule is to prioritise** prominent organisations recognised for their engagement in AI ethics and governance, those contributing to public debates on the social implications of AI, those engaging in research and knowledge creation, those into policy advocacy or holding government to account, and those providing support for AI-related initiatives. This questionnaire collects evidence across **all indicators** (primary and secondary) in the Ethics and Sustainability dimension.

    Begin by scanning through the webpages of prominent CSOs working on AI in your country. From your search results, look out for other organisations collaborating in this area.

    #### **Useful Sources**

    * The websites of human rights agencies, civil society organisations, unions, and labour organisations or collectives that work on this dimension
    * Academic publications and reports from reputable (I)NGOs
    * Websites of credible news outlets
    * Government websites for evidence of engagements with civil society organisations.
    * Websites of and reports of UN Agencies and International Organisations (see e.g. UNESCO’s [website](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/es/node/48/list) of Global CSOs and Academic Network on AI Ethics and Policy)

    #### **Search**

    * News and articles about recent developments and reforms in relation to “AI” \+ “environmental impact”, “transparency & explainability”, “human oversight & determination”, “fairness & non-discrimination” \+ “Country”
    * You can conduct speculative searches using the key words in each initiative type, e.g. “CSO organises capacity building on AI and environmental rights in Trinidad and Tobago” “Organisation takes legal action against the New Zealand government on transparency and explainability of government AI systems”
    * General google searches for ‘CSOs working on AI Ethics and Sustainability \[country\]’, ‘AI and the environment \[country\]’, etc. (can also include terms ‘NGOs’, ‘CSOs’, ‘Organisation’, etc.)
    * Academic search engines ([Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com/), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/), [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/), etc.) for ‘CSO reports on AI Transparency and Explainability \[country\]

    #### **Consult**

    * Experts, civil society organisations and researchers working on digital rights and AI, specifically in relation to transparency, oversight, explainability, and the environment
